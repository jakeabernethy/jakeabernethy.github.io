Longer academic bio

Jacob Abernethy is a Research Scientist at Google Research and an Associate Professor in the School of Computer Science at Georgia Tech. He holds a 20% appointment at Georgia Tech, where he continues to advise PhD students and postdoctoral researchers, collaborate with colleagues, and occasionally teach courses.

His research lies at the intersection of machine learning, optimization, statistics, economics, and game theory. Much of his academic work has focused on online learning, learning in games, and the behavior of adaptive decision-making systems. At Google, his work has centered primarily on large language models. Since January 2025, he has been a major contributor to the Gemini Large-Scale Pretraining team, with work spanning mixture-of-experts architectures, model sparsity, and large-scale optimization.

Before joining Georgia Tech, Abernethy was a faculty member at the University of Michigan. He earned his PhD in Computer Science from UC Berkeley, where he was advised by Peter Bartlett, and subsequently spent two years as a Simons Postdoctoral Fellow at the University of Pennsylvania, working with Michael Kearns.

Abernethy is also a co-founder and board member of BlueConduit, a company that grew out of research applying machine learning to the identification of lead service lines during the Flint water crisis. Across his academic, industry, and entrepreneurial work, he has moved between the theoretical foundations of learning and optimization, public-interest data science, and the development of large-scale AI systems.

From Flint to BlueConduit

Jacob co-founded BlueConduit with Eric Schwartz. The company develops AI and software tools for water systems, but its origin story is unusual: an NSF research-and-education project became a student data-science team; that team became a volunteer civic partnership during a public-health crisis; and that partnership eventually became a company.

In 2015, Jacob received an NSF CAREER award for research connecting machine learning and microeconomic theory. One educational component was a new student initiative that grew into the Michigan Data Science Team, co-organized with Eric. MDST expanded rapidly, pairing University of Michigan students with applied projects for outside partners. In 2016, a Google.org grant supported MyWater-Flint, a resident-facing app and website developed by U-M researchers and MDST students. The team’s risk assessments helped residents and officials make sense of fragmented data during the Flint water crisis.

The team developed statistical and machine-learning models to estimate which homes were most likely to have lead or galvanized service lines requiring replacement. An early public video explained the approach, and the research produced two KDD papers: A Data Science Approach to Understanding Residential Water Contamination in Flint in 2017 and ActiveRemediation: The Search for Lead Pipes in Flint, Michigan in 2018. The latter received the KDD Best Student Paper Award in the Applied Data Science track.

The work quickly moved beyond research. Jacob, Eric, and doctoral student Jared Webb provided volunteer data-science and software support to Flint’s Fast Start pipe-replacement program, using the model to help select addresses for excavation and replacement. In 2018, however, AECOM and the city stopped using the model to guide digs amid a disputed transition and competing political and operational priorities. Reporting later showed that the rate at which excavations found hazardous lines fell from roughly 80 percent to 14 percent, while meeting records revealed tensions between efficient targeting and contractor incentives. In 2019, as part of the ongoing federal settlement, the city agreed to return to the predictive model and use it to prioritize as many as 5,200 addresses with the highest likelihood of lead or galvanized service lines.

After The Atlantic told the story nationally, the team began hearing from other municipalities confronting the same problem. The EPA later cited ActiveRemediation in its 2022 guidance on service-line inventories, identifying Flint as a successful example of predictive modeling. Jacob and Eric founded BlueConduit in 2019 to make these tools available to water systems beyond Flint. BlueConduit now reports that it has inventoried 5.8 million service lines across 330 communities in the United States and Canada, serving more than 18 million people. Its work has also expanded beyond lead service lines to include water-main failure-risk prediction.

Beyond the lab

Before academic life became mostly papers, talks, and students, Jacob performed comedy juggling shows, won “Funniest Student at UMass,” and later opened for Sinbad and Dave Chappelle.

Beyond research, he has worked with MEET: Middle East Entrepreneurs of Tomorrow, completed a Boston-to-San-Francisco bicycle ride with Keith Vanderlinde and Damien Burke, and spent a lot of time playing squash and racquetball.
